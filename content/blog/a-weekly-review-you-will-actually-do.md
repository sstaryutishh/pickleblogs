A planning system becomes less useful when it stops reflecting reality. Tasks remain on the list after they no longer matter. Dates move. Notes contain commitments that never made it into a task manager. A weekly review is a small appointment for bringing those things back into agreement.

It does not need an elaborate ritual. Start with fifteen minutes, the tools you already use, and three questions: what is still open, what is coming, and what deserves attention next?

## Gather the loose ends

Open the places where commitments tend to accumulate: a task inbox, a notes inbox, your calendar, and any messages you marked for follow-up. You are looking for obligations, rather than attempting to organize every piece of information you have collected.

Write each actionable item as a concrete next step. “Website” is a subject. “Send the revised homepage copy to the designer” is an action. The second gives your future self enough information to begin.

If an item is no longer relevant, remove it. Keeping an abandoned intention on a list does not make it more likely to matter again.

## Look backward briefly

Review the week that just ended. Check for a meeting that produced a follow-up, an unfinished promise, or a decision that changed a project’s direction.

This is not an exercise in scoring yourself. It is a way to catch information that might otherwise stay in memory. If a task repeatedly remained unfinished, ask whether it was too vague, too large, or less important than you thought.

Change the task accordingly. Breaking it into a smaller next action can help when the obstacle is ambiguity. Removing it can help when the obstacle is that it no longer deserves your time.

## Look forward realistically

Read the next week’s calendar before assigning more work. Meetings, travel, appointments, and personal commitments already occupy space. A plan that ignores them begins with an impossible assumption.

Notice anything that needs preparation. Put that preparation before the event, with enough room to complete it. A reminder at the moment of a meeting is useful for attendance; it is too late to become preparation time.

Leave room for ordinary uncertainty. If every hour has a job, a small change can make the whole plan feel broken.

## Choose a few outcomes

Select two or three outcomes that would make the coming week worthwhile. Then identify the next action for each. An outcome describes what changes; an action describes what you will do.

For example, “Make the proposal ready for review” might begin with “Draft the scope section.” The distinction helps you connect a small piece of work to a useful result without pretending you can finish everything in one sitting.

Put those actions somewhere you already look. The review should improve your existing system, rather than create another list to maintain.

## End when the review is useful

You can stop when your commitments are visible, the next week feels plausible, and you know where to begin. There will always be another folder to tidy or a label to refine.
